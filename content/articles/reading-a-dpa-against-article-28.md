## What the regulation requires

Article 28(3) requires a binding contract that sets out the subject matter, duration, nature and purpose of the processing, the type of personal data and the categories of data subjects. It then lists terms the processor must accept.

The processor must act only on the controller's documented instructions, and must tell the controller if an instruction would breach data protection law. Everyone authorised to handle the data must be bound by confidentiality. The processor must apply the security measures required by Article 32.

Sub-processors may be engaged only with the controller's authorisation, and the same obligations must flow down to them. The processor must help the controller answer data subject requests and meet its obligations on security, breach notification and impact assessments. At the end of the service it must delete or return the data. And it must make available the information needed to show compliance, including allowing audits.

A useful first pass is to check the agreement against that list term by term. A missing item is a defect in the contract, not a point to haggle over.

## Where negotiation actually happens

The regulation fixes the subjects but leaves the mechanics open, and that is where agreements differ. Sub-processor changes are the clearest case: how much notice the controller gets, and whether it can object and what happens if it does. A notice period too short to review a new vendor makes the authorisation formal only.

Audit rights are the second. A controller will want the ability to verify; a processor serving thousands of customers will offer independent reports instead of site visits. A workable middle is reports first, with an audit available where the reports leave a specific question open.

Breach notice is the third. Article 33(2) requires a processor to notify the controller without undue delay, and a controller with its own deadline to the regulator needs a contractual number of hours that it can meet. Transfers outside the EEA, usually handled through standard contractual clauses, belong in the same review rather than a separate one.

## Roles come first

Before any clause, confirm that the parties are labelled correctly. A vendor that decides why and how data is used is a controller for that use, whatever the contract calls it. A mislabelled role produces a document that satisfies Article 28 on paper and describes the wrong relationship.
