In his second year of college he fell for a laugh. It belonged to Meera, who sat two rows ahead in the lecture hall, always on the aisle so she could leave the minute the bell went. She laughed easily and loudly, and the people around her smiled before they knew what the joke was. He could pick it out from the far end of a corridor.

He started coming to class early so he could get a seat the laugh would reach. He timed his walks between lectures to pass the banyan tree where she and her friends sat. He noticed that she used her hands when she talked, and that she tucked a loose strand of hair behind her ear when she needed a second to think. He knew which pen she used. He told himself this was not stalking, since he never followed her anywhere, and then he told himself to stop explaining it to himself.

He practised in the hostel bathroom, in front of a mirror with a crack running across one corner. "I like you," he told his reflection. "Would you like to have coffee?" He tried longer versions. He tried a joke and took it out again. He timed the whole speech against the traffic light at the junction outside campus, so that it would fit inside one red signal. He wanted to be ready in case his courage came and went before he could use it.

For a week he tried to arrange a moment. He went to the library when she was there and pretended to look for a book, staring at spines he couldn't read. He stood in the canteen queue three people behind her. He memorised the name on her notebook, as if knowing it would bring the world round to his side. It didn't. She was always with her friends when his courage showed up, and by the time she was alone it had wandered off again.

His roommate Ashwin worked it out in two days, mainly because Arun stopped eating the hostel egg curry, which he normally defended against all complaints. "Is it the girl from Economics?" Ashwin asked. Arun said it was nothing. "She's out of your league, da," said Ashwin, kindly, the way you tell someone the bus has already left. Then he gave Arun half of his own omelette, which in that hostel was close to a vow of friendship. Arun ate it, grateful, and felt if anything slightly more invisible. To be understood that quickly by someone who wasn't even trying meant he must look very plain indeed.

On a Thursday evening the sky came down low and the rain broke all at once. Students scattered under eaves and into doorways. Meera was standing at the end of the corridor of the economics block, looking out at the water with an expression he couldn't read. People ran past her shouting. She was by herself.

He felt his courage come up like a hand breaking the surface of water. He walked over from the corner where he'd been pretending to check his phone. He told himself it was the smallest request in the world. A coffee. An hour. People did it all the time. If he couldn't ask for that, he didn't know what he thought he was asking life for.

"Meera," he said. Hearing her name in his own voice felt like doing an impression of somebody braver. She turned, expecting a question about the timetable.

"Can I tell you something?" His throat didn't trust itself with a second try.

"Okay," she said, curious and in no hurry. She tapped her notebook with her fingers.

"I like you," he said. "Would you want to get a coffee sometime?"

For a second he thought he saw her flinch. It could have been the rain. It could have been her face looking for the right expression. She tilted her head and gave him the kindest smile she had that was still honest.

"Arun, you're sweet. But I've never thought of you that way. Not even once."

The tiny pause after *sweet*. Then *not even once*, coming down like a stamp on paper. She shifted the strap of her bag and walked into the rain, and the water closed around her head and shoulders, and then she was just another shape among the umbrellas. He stayed under the eaves with a notebook and a sentence.

He didn't cry on the way back to the hostel. The rain was doing that for him. The notebook in his bag went soft and the ink bled, and when he emptied it over the dustbin by the stairs the pages came out in grey lumps.

That night he lay on his bed and watched the fan cut the air into four. He went over her sentence carefully, because it deserved care. She hadn't said *I don't feel the same way*. She hadn't said *maybe not now*, or *maybe later*. She had said *not even once*, and he couldn't help admiring how efficient it was. It told him he had never existed in her mind as a possibility. He had been a boy from her class, near the door, a name she'd remember at exam time, someone who would hold a seat if she asked. He had not been someone a door could open for.

The next morning he went to class. Meera came in laughing with a friend. He didn't change his seat. He didn't look at her, and he didn't pretend not to look at her either. He took notes in a new notebook, with great attention, in handwriting that looked better than it ever had. When the lecture ended he waited until the corridor emptied and then walked out. The rain had washed everything. The campus smelled of wet earth. He looked up at the banyan and its leaves were shining, with the stunned brightness of things that have just been scrubbed.

For the next week he watched himself like a scientist with a sensitive specimen. He counted how many times an hour he thought of her and tried to bring it down by one each hour. He practised not checking the door when he heard her laugh. He taught himself to cross the courtyard like a person with somewhere to be. When his friends asked why he was so quiet he said he was worried about an exam. Conversations went over him the way small waves go over a stone, and then flattened out on the sand.

On the tenth day the count had dropped by two. He called it progress, not because it was measurable but because he needed something to call it. The scar was small and neat. He could carry it.
