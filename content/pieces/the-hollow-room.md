## Part I: Edges of a Life

Arun was never really noticed, and it began so early that he could not have said when. Nobody did anything to him. That was the trouble. If somebody had, there would have been something to point at.

In Class Four the boys chose sides for cricket every afternoon. Two captains, two lines, the same ritual each day. The good ones went in the first minute: the fast bowler who grinned at everything, the boy who could hit the ball over the compound wall, the loud one who argued with every decision and still scored when it mattered. Then the middle went. Then four or five of them were left standing around, pretending they didn't mind. Arun was usually among them. Somebody would count heads, sigh, and say, "Okay, Arun, you come this side." Nobody was cruel about it. Nobody was happy about it either.

He learned to follow a game by its sound. The shouting rose a little whenever something was about to happen, and it almost never rose for him. One day he got to bat. The bowler lobbed the ball with a smirk and the fielders drifted about lazily. Arun hit it cleanly. He felt the thump run up through his wrists and watched the ball roll past a fielder who was looking at something else. Then he looked up and waited for someone to cheer. Two boys near the boundary were arguing about last week's match. The bowler asked whether that counted as a hit. "Play on," said the captain, bored already.

Around that time the class teacher asked everyone to bring a family photograph for the noticeboard. By the next morning the board was full of weddings, cousins, grandmothers in shawls, birthday cakes, brothers in cricket whites. Arun brought a small matte photo from a studio near the bus stand. His parents stood on either side of him with a hand on each of his shoulders, all three of them stiff in front of a painted backdrop of clouds and a rainbow. The teacher pinned the photographs up in rows, with a blue ribbon running along the top. His ended up in the bottom corner, slightly crooked, just past the point where the ribbon stopped. He straightened the pin after school, when the room was empty.

Birthdays at home were small. His mother bought a round cake with a pink border and one plastic rose in the middle. The lighter never worked on the first click. His father hummed a film song while he cut paper plates out of old newspaper. One year Arun invited five boys from his class. Two were going out of station. One's mother didn't allow him out in the evenings. One had tuition. One said he would come and didn't. "It's raining," his mother said, and put her hand on his hair, as if the rain explained everything. In the end the cake was cut with cousins. He smiled for the photographs. It was a small cake, and it was still too much cake.

He turned out to be good at other people's birthdays. He remembered every one of them, and every year he wrote the message before anyone else did, with the right number of exclamation marks. The replies came back quickly: "Thanks da!" with a smiley. By evening his message was buried under forty others, and he didn't mind, because that was how it worked. You were a name on a list that people were glad to see once a year.

In Class Seven the teacher stopped at his bench during roll call, frowned at her chart, and asked the room, "Who sits here again?" Everyone laughed, the easy laugh of a class that has found a moment that doesn't matter. Somebody told her his name. She nodded and moved on. For days afterwards the question kept coming back to him at odd times. Who sits here again. It didn't hurt exactly. It made him feel thinner, as if his outline was a pencil drawing someone had started to rub out.

When he was twelve there was a class picnic. He took the bus across town with a bottle of water sweating in his bag and a list of things his mother had made him check twice. At the park everyone ran towards a fountain shaped like a fish. He waited for someone to call him, and nobody did, so he walked there alone and stopped once to shake a stone out of his shoe. For the scavenger hunt the teacher told them to form groups of five. By the time a boy waved him over, the groups had already counted themselves. "Sorry, full," the boy said, and went back to talking about a clue shaped like a leaf. The teacher made a new group out of whoever was left. They hadn't been picked. They had been placed. There is a difference, and he noticed it every time.

At the annual day in Class Nine he was given the job of pulling the curtain. It was an important job, the teacher said, and he believed her until the play began. For forty minutes he stood in the wings with a rope in his hands and watched the others take their bows. When the curtain finally closed on the last scene, he was the only person backstage with nothing to take off, no costume, no make-up, nobody to hug. His mother said later that she could see him from the third row and that he'd done it beautifully. He thanked her. It was the most anyone had said about it.

Growing up didn't fix anything. He watched the others move into the language of being young, and he watched like a visitor who can follow the grammar but can't speak. The boys whispered about girls and ribbed each other and respected each other in the same breath. The girls laughed and shoved each other's shoulders. Folded notes went around inside textbooks, and hands touched a little longer than a joke needed. Arun laughed when others laughed. He copied expressions carefully, the way a student copies a diagram off the board. He wasn't an outcast and he wasn't in. He stood where the floor continues past the edge of the carpet.

He started a small notebook of things other people seemed to know without being taught. What to talk about at break. When to cut into a conversation without breaking its rhythm. Questions that sound interested without sounding hungry. He practised saying "How was your test?" and then leaving a pause for the answer to turn into a conversation. The answers never did. They dropped like biscuit crumbs and stayed where they fell.

At fifteen he watched a film alone for the first time. He told his parents it was a class outing. He stood in the ticket queue between two boys complaining about the hero's new hairstyle and a group of girls comparing earrings. The man at the counter tore his ticket without looking up. Inside, the back rows were full of couples, and the laughter in there sounded like bees. When the lights came on, the hall emptied in two directions. Arun stayed in his seat a few seconds longer than he needed to. It wasn't sadness. It was closer to taking notes.

At seventeen a girl from his class asked him for a favour. "Can you stand in the corridor and shout if Ma'am comes? I need to return a book and I don't want her to know I read it during maths." She smiled the way people smile when they are sure you won't complicate their day. He said yes. He stood there watching the corridor and the girl at once, hoping for a second request that would carry some warmth. The teacher turned the corner. He called out. The girl dashed back, thanked him while already moving past, and handed the book over with a perfect smile. He had been a hinge. Doors swung on him.

By the time school ended, the facts of his life had set into something simple. People liked him. People didn't choose him. The two were not the same, and he knew exactly where the line ran. He took it with him to college, the way you carry a scar on your back: you know it's there, but you can't see it without a mirror. He didn't resent anyone. He wasn't built for resentment. He was built for putting up with things.

## Part II: The First Confession

In his second year of college he fell for a laugh. It belonged to Meera, who sat two rows ahead in the lecture hall, always on the aisle so she could leave the minute the bell went. She laughed easily and loudly, and the people around her smiled before they knew what the joke was. He could pick it out from the far end of a corridor.

He started coming to class early so he could get a seat the laugh would reach. He timed his walks between lectures to pass the banyan tree where she and her friends sat. He noticed that she used her hands when she talked, and that she tucked a loose strand of hair behind her ear when she needed a second to think. He knew which pen she used. He told himself this was not stalking, since he never followed her anywhere, and then he told himself to stop explaining it to himself.

He practised in the hostel bathroom, in front of a mirror with a crack running across one corner. "I like you," he told his reflection. "Would you like to have coffee?" He tried longer versions. He tried a joke and took it out again. He timed the whole speech against the traffic light at the junction outside campus, so that it would fit inside one red signal. He wanted to be ready in case his courage came and went before he could use it.

For a week he tried to arrange a moment. He went to the library when she was there and pretended to look for a book, staring at spines he couldn't read. He stood in the canteen queue three people behind her. He memorised the name on her notebook, as if knowing it would bring the world round to his side. It didn't. She was always with her friends when his courage showed up, and by the time she was alone it had wandered off again.

His roommate Ashwin worked it out in two days, mainly because Arun stopped eating the hostel egg curry, which he normally defended against all complaints. "Is it the girl from Economics?" Ashwin asked. Arun said it was nothing. "She's out of your league, da," said Ashwin, kindly, the way you tell someone the bus has already left. Then he gave Arun half of his own omelette, which in that hostel was close to a vow of friendship. Arun ate it, grateful, and felt if anything slightly more invisible. To be understood that quickly by someone who wasn't even trying meant he must look very plain indeed.

On a Thursday evening the sky came down low and the rain broke all at once. Students scattered under eaves and into doorways. Meera was standing at the end of the corridor of the economics block, looking out at the water with an expression he couldn't read. People ran past her shouting. She was by herself.

He felt his courage come up like a hand breaking the surface of water. He walked over from the corner where he'd been pretending to check his phone. He told himself it was the smallest request in the world. A coffee. An hour. People did it all the time. If he couldn't ask for that, he didn't know what he thought he was asking life for.

"Meera," he said. Hearing her name in his own voice felt like doing an impression of somebody braver. She turned, expecting a question about the timetable.

"Can I tell you something?" His throat didn't trust itself with a second try.

"Okay," she said, curious and in no hurry. She tapped her notebook with her fingers.

"I like you," he said. "Would you want to get a coffee sometime?"

For a second he thought he saw her flinch. It could have been the rain. It could have been her face looking for the right expression. She tilted her head and gave him the kindest smile she had that was still honest.

"Arun, you're sweet. But I've never thought of you that way. Not even once."

The tiny pause after *sweet*. Then *not even once*, coming down like a stamp on paper. She shifted the strap of her bag and walked into the rain, and the water closed around her head and shoulders, and then she was just another shape among the umbrellas. He stayed under the eaves with a notebook and a sentence.

He didn't cry on the way back to the hostel. The rain was doing that for him. The notebook in his bag went soft and the ink bled, and when he emptied it over the dustbin by the stairs the pages came out in grey lumps.

That night he lay on his bed and watched the fan cut the air into four. He went over her sentence carefully, because it deserved care. She hadn't said *I don't feel the same way*. She hadn't said *maybe not now*, or *maybe later*. She had said *not even once*, and he couldn't help admiring how efficient it was. It told him he had never existed in her mind as a possibility. He had been a boy from her class, near the door, a name she'd remember at exam time, someone who would hold a seat if she asked. He had not been someone a door could open for.

The next morning he went to class. Meera came in laughing with a friend. He didn't change his seat. He didn't look at her, and he didn't pretend not to look at her either. He took notes in a new notebook, with great attention, in handwriting that looked better than it ever had. When the lecture ended he waited until the corridor emptied and then walked out. The rain had washed everything. The campus smelled of wet earth. He looked up at the banyan and its leaves were shining, with the stunned brightness of things that have just been scrubbed.

For the next week he watched himself like a scientist with a sensitive specimen. He counted how many times an hour he thought of her and tried to bring it down by one each hour. He practised not checking the door when he heard her laugh. He taught himself to cross the courtyard like a person with somewhere to be. When his friends asked why he was so quiet he said he was worried about an exam. Conversations went over him the way small waves go over a stone, and then flattened out on the sand.

On the tenth day the count had dropped by two. He called it progress, not because it was measurable but because he needed something to call it. The scar was small and neat. He could carry it.

## Part III: The Second Confession

Adulthood turned out to be a beige room with a lot of computers humming in it. At twenty-seven Arun was an analyst, a title that suggested rigour and hid a great deal of copy-paste. He had a spreadsheet open, a report template saved under a name he never changed, and a reputation for being dependable. People asked him for numbers when they needed to impress someone else, and he gave them numbers, and the numbers behaved.

The office smelled of air-conditioning and instant coffee. The corridor carpet had a pattern designed to hide stains, which it did very well. Three times a week the snack trolley came round at four with a clatter of cups and a spoon banging against a flask, and people stood up from their cubicles as if someone had called their names. Jokes made the rounds the same way. You could hear one at the far end of the floor, slightly changed, ten minutes after you'd heard it near the pantry. Arun laughed in the right places. He kept a few sugar sachets lined up on his desk, on the theory that it gave him a personality.

Kavya sat in the next row of cubicles. She wasn't someone who made an entrance. She simply made the spot where she stood a little warmer. Her hair clip never matched her earrings. She brought snacks in zip-lock pouches and offered them to whoever was within reach. She asked ordinary questions, and people answered with far more detail than the questions had asked for. "How was your weekend?" and someone would end up showing her photographs of a child at a fountain. "Did you watch that new series?" and a man would re-enact a whole scene in the corridor. One afternoon she held the pouch out to Arun. "Try. It's spicy, careful." A mix of sev and peanuts and dried peas.

The spice was a small ceremony. He coughed once, for form's sake, and she laughed. It wasn't Meera's laugh. It was a practical one, the kind that tells you the room is still in one piece.

In the first month he thanked her for the snacks and went back to his spreadsheet, a little ashamed of himself. In the second month his attention began to drift. He caught himself waiting for four o'clock. He built small arrangements of peanuts at the corner of his desk, a triangle, then a square. He started asking about her weekends in the careful way of a man learning a language late in life. She told him about a cousin's engagement and a market where lilies were sold tied up with kitchen twine. He listened the way you listen when you're hoping there will be room in the story for you.

He didn't confuse friendliness with something else. He told himself that regularly, like a drill. People were touchy about being misread, and he knew it. But when her sleeve brushed his arm as she pointed to a cell on his screen, his heart jumped and then acted as if nothing had happened. He learned when she took her tea breaks and found reasons to be near the pantry at the same time. He rehearsed again, but these weren't confessions. They were smaller things, ways of asking for a little of her time. "There's a new café near the metro, want to try?" "I've been curious about that vegetarian place." He tried to imagine the question in different corridors, so he could pick the one where it would sound least like trespassing.

One evening at the end of the quarter, the office emptied early because people with families were running for trains that wouldn't wait. The light went grey and soft and made the carpet look less aggressive. Kavya was squinting at a chart that would be changed again before morning. Arun stood up, sat down, stood up again, and reminded himself that nothing happens when you do nothing. Then he walked to the edge of her cubicle.

"Kavya."

She looked up with a neutral smile that could have turned into anything. He held on to the partition because his hands wanted a job.

"I was thinking," he said, "if you'd like to go out sometime. Dinner, maybe."

The moment gathered into one dense point, and everything had to pass through it. He watched her face reorganise itself. The friendliness didn't disappear. It stepped back one pace.

"Arun," she said, and his name already sounded like the first line of a policy. "Don't misunderstand. I'm just being friendly. Please don't make this awkward."

She turned back to her screen and tapped out three firm keystrokes that didn't mean anything. The air between them had changed, and neither of them did anything about it.

He stayed there long enough for it to look silly. "Of course," he said, though he wasn't sure what he was agreeing to, and walked back to his desk slowly, as if slow would make less noise.

The next day the pouch did not come across the corridor. She didn't look over her monitor to ask how he'd slept. She talked to the others about a TV serial, in the same practical laugh, which was no longer meant for him. He went to refill his water bottle twice and came back with it half full both times, because some errands have to be made to look longer than they are.

Praveen from procurement, who had opinions on everything, found him at the water cooler that week. "Don't take it personally," he said. "She's nice to everyone. It's practically in her appraisal." Arun said he wasn't taking it personally. "Good, good," said Praveen, who could clearly tell he was, and went off to tell somebody else.

At lunch Arun ate faster than usual and felt overfull. He told himself he hadn't been humiliated. He had been corrected. He thought about the word *awkward* and admired it. It pointed at a discomfort that had nothing to do with pain, and it kept everything on the surface and made the surface slippery.

Over the weekend he tried to reset himself. He ironed his shirts and lined them up by shade, lightest to darkest. He cleaned the one shelf where he kept his books. He went to the market and bought coriander, because coriander brightens a kitchen even when it wilts. He watched a cricket match and let the commentary do his thinking. During the ad break he muted the TV and listened to the ceiling. The silence inside the mute was different from the silence of no sound. It had the weight of something taken away.

On Monday he went back to the role, which he had learned well. The office, the corridor, the trolley, the sugar sachets. He gave Kavya a nod with exactly the right dose of neutral. A colleague showed her a photograph of a child at a fountain, again, and Arun looked at it and said "Cute," in the tone he'd borrowed from a hundred earlier approvals. The child had an orange-stained mouth. He wondered what snack had done it, and whether anyone had told the child he was adorable at that moment, or whether the adults had waited until there was a camera.

He didn't write any of this in a notebook. He didn't tell anyone that his heart had lurched once and then fixed its posture. The office had swallowed bigger sorrows than his without a mark on the carpet. He took the lesson it was offering. Be legible. Don't take up more air than you need.

## Part IV: The Third Confession

At thirty-one he felt like a man wheeling a bicycle through a lane with traffic in both directions, fast enough to make decisions complicated and slow enough to make hurrying look silly. He wasn't defeated. He wouldn't have dramatised it if he were. He was practical, careful, employed. He rang his parents every other week and sent his mother a link to a cheaper pressure cooker. He fixed a leaking tap himself. He switched tea brands when the shopkeeper told him the new one was fresher. All of this counted as a life.

Asha arrived in it without any ceremony. She worked in another vertical and came into his orbit through a shared project, then stayed at its edge after the project ended. They found out they liked the same old film for different reasons. She loved the way the heroine held a silence; he loved the scene where the hero walks across a field and there is no music. She ate quickly and quietly and finished her plate before she finished her sentence. She listened with her chin on her fist, which made her look more serious than the subject needed. When she laughed at one of his small jokes he felt warm, and the warmth didn't make him stupid. It made him tidy.

They started having lunch together when the tables weren't full. They never agreed to it. If she was there he sat across from her; if she wasn't, he ate alone. He didn't plan his day around hers. He respected the discretion of chance.

She asked questions that felt like presents you could actually hold. "What did you read as a child," she asked once, "and which of those books would you avoid rereading, so they don't shrink?" Nobody had ever asked him that. He told her about a book of stories from the library near his house, the blue stamp inside the cover, the smell a book gets when many hands have turned it slowly. He said he wouldn't reread it. He wanted it to stay the size it was. She nodded as if he'd said something that made sense.

In the second month she said, "Shall we try that café on the corner? The one that thinks it's French." They went after work. The café had yellow bulbs in little glass cages. They sat by the window with two cups of tea in mugs with long handles that made you hold them wrong. She told him about a cousin who refused to wear shoes at a wedding because they pinched his toes. He told her about the lizard behind his fridge that came out only when he left a slice of banana, to test whether lizards eat fruit. They don't, in his experience.

After that the café became theirs, in the loose way things were ever theirs. She argued with the counter boy about the price of a croissant that wasn't one. He learned that she took her tea without sugar and then stole his biscuit. Sometimes they sat for an hour and said almost nothing, and he found that this didn't frighten him. He kept waiting for the old tightness in his chest, the feeling of being a guest in a stranger's house, and it kept not arriving.

It occurred to him that affection didn't have to be a room you rushed into. It could be a chair you kept coming back to because it fitted your back better than the others. He wasn't a boy talking to a mirror any more. He was a man who could name what he wanted without swallowing the person in front of him. He was quietly proud of that.

The day he decided to say it was an ordinary Tuesday. Ordinary is the best container for a decision that might fail. They went to the small café near the office, since the big one had been chaos all week. The boy at the counter knew Asha's order and smiled at her. The steel counter reflected their faces as thin streaks. They sat at a table against the wall, under a fake chalkboard listing the specials in English, with the confidence of someone who has never been corrected in public.

He could have waited. He could have let another month of conversations pile up like plates. But he had learned that patience can turn into an apology for fear, and that fear sets traps in whatever time you give it. He didn't want to be trapped. He also didn't want to be ridiculous.

"Asha," he said as they put their cups down, "I need to tell you something."

She looked up with the patient attention that made people say more than they'd planned. He could see the two of them in the steel behind the counter, faint and small.

"I like you," he said. "More than as a friend."

The silence that followed took real time. He watched it fill the table. He had prepared himself to be refused. He hadn't prepared for how gently a refusal could be arranged.

She reached across and put her hand over his. It was light and warm and careful. It reminded him of a towel pressed on a bruise.

"Arun," she said, "I'm sorry. I care about you. I do. But not like that. You deserve someone who sees you that way, and I don't."

He nodded as if she'd given him directions to a place he had no plans to visit. "I understand," he said, because understanding was what he had always offered whenever he was asked for anything. He didn't take his hand away until she did. He lifted his mug and burnt his tongue, because the tea was hotter than he'd expected. He smiled. She smiled. They talked about a problem at work the way you talk about a neighbour's dog that has run into your house. The conversation stayed calm. The problem wasn't his or hers. It was something someone else could fix.

On the walk back to the office he let her do the talking. He noticed that his legs had picked a pace which made her take a half-step quicker than usual, so he slowed down. She asked him about something that used to interest him and he answered like a man reading an old signboard.

That night he sat on the edge of his cot and counted the loudest sounds in his room. The fan. A horn outside. A neighbour dragging a bucket across the bathroom floor. He tried to give each sound exactly one thought, so that the thoughts would stay countable. They didn't. Thoughts multiply like mould once you let moisture in, and he had let it in.

The week after that, they had lunch twice, then once, then not at all. Nobody was punishing anybody. It was only the balance sorting itself out. When they did cross paths they smiled and traded small sentences, light enough to hold in the mouth. He felt grateful, and oddly relieved. Kindness had done the cruellest thing it can. It had taken away his right to be angry.

On Sunday he went to the market and bought a packet of cream-coloured writing paper. He told himself it was for a letter to an aunt. He didn't write to the aunt. He opened the packet and ran his fingers over the top sheet. It had faint ruled lines you could only see at a certain angle. They felt like rules you accept because you're tired of improvising.

## Part V: The Flat

By thirty-four, Arun knew the sound a day makes when it is exactly like yesterday. First came the milk truck, a clatter of crates outside the shop downstairs. Then the tap, which he left running into a little puddle at its base that never quite reached his toes. He folded his bedding into a neat rectangle and slid it under the cot. The cot sagged in the middle, because bodies have weight and beds remember.

The flat had one window, and it looked on to a lane where the two-wheeler horns had settled on three notes. Across the lane stood a building with balconies stacked like boxes, each one a slightly different arrangement of the same life: a clothes-rack, a bicycle, a plastic chair, a plant. One plant, with tired leaves, sat on a balcony for a few months and then turned up on another, as if it had changed tenants. The glass in his own window had a crack that ran diagonally from the upper left corner. He couldn't remember when it appeared. It went most of the way down, stopped, then veered slightly off. Some mornings he traced it with his finger, as if you could steer a day to a different place by following a line.

The room was a collection of careful shortages. One cot. One desk with a chipped edge that snagged threads from his shirt. One chair with a wobble that a folded newspaper under one leg could fix. One plate, one steel tumbler, one pan, one knife with a handle repaired with cloth tape. A single gas burner that needed a twist of paper to catch. It wasn't a style. It was arithmetic. Fewer things meant fewer questions, and fewer questions meant fewer reminders that nobody else was there.

He cooked twice a week, in a way that sounded like care and tasted of salt. Rice. A dal that swung between too thin and too thick. A potato fry he was fond of because potatoes agreed not to be complicated. Sometimes he added a slice of tomato just to watch it go soft. He ate at the desk and listened to the city breathing in and out. A plate for one is easy to wash. He dried it with a cloth that used to be a pillowcase and then laid the cloth over the window grille, so the sun could take whatever damp the fibres still held.

On the wall opposite the window, a stain had grown over the years. From a certain angle it looked like an island seen from a plane. He gave it a name once, forgot the name, and never gave it another. He kept meaning to scrub it with soap and didn't, because the stain gave the wall a story, and his body couldn't.

His mother rang on Sundays at eleven. She asked whether he was eating properly, which meant whether he was eating rice, and he said yes. She told him about the neighbours' daughter's wedding, the price of coconut, his father's knee. At the end she always said, in the same tone, "You should think about settling down," and he said, "Yes, Amma, I'll think," and they both let the sentence go by, like a bus neither of them meant to catch. After the call he sat for a while with the phone in his hand, until the screen went dark by itself.

Saturdays were the longest. The office gave him a reason to be somewhere, and without it the day had to be built from nothing. He did laundry. He swept. He went to the market and bought exactly what he needed and came home early with it, like a man who'd been told the shop would close. By three in the afternoon there was nothing left to arrange, and he would sit with a book open on his knee and listen to the building: someone's pressure cooker whistling, someone's child learning a scale on a keyboard, a quarrel two floors up that ended in a laugh.

At night he sat in the chair by the window and watched the lane turn into a display of other people's closeness. The tea stall at the corner clinked its cups. The TV in the shop across the road showed a match, and the cheers from it reached him a few seconds late, because the broadcast ran behind the world. People on balconies leaned towards one another in postures that said what words would have made theatrical. Couples on motorbikes rested their palms on each other's waists. A child laughed an octave above everybody. The lane held all of it without comment. It didn't flaunt it. It just showed him what people do when no one is making them do anything.

He rested his hands on the window sill, the way you rest them on a temple railing. He didn't feel any prayer rise. What he felt was listening, too careful to be called longing. The glass cooled his fingertips. He left his fingerprints there until the night warmed them away.

He measured his evenings by the ceiling fan. Each rotation had a tiny falter at the point where one blade bent a little more than the rest. He heard it the way you hear someone who repeats a word when they're nervous. He began looking forward to the moment he switched the fan off, because for a few seconds the silence held the room without any walls to it. Then he'd switch it on again, because comfort is often just a noise you've agreed to.

On some days he stayed late at the office to avoid being the first one to turn a key in a lock. The fluorescent lights did a fine job of suggesting nobody needed anything beyond light. He filled his water bottle and drank half, because half looked like moderation. He replied to an email that would have been fine unanswered. He updated a spreadsheet that didn't care. By the time he got home the lane had already collected everyone else's day and hung it out like laundry.

He didn't bring people to the flat. If colleagues suggested drinks, he declined as if he had already promised somebody else. A few times they talked him into going, and he sat at the edge of the table smiling while someone told a story about a pub that overcharged for the same beer. The stories were polished to a small radius. He admired them like gemstones in a shop window. He felt nothing glitter. When they asked if he had anything to share he said he had nothing new, and they were kind enough not to insist.

He had three work shirts. He washed them on Saturday, ironed them on Sunday and lined up the seams, because a straight line makes you look as though you've planned something. One pair of shoes. Two pairs of trousers that took turns being the good pair. A belt with a worn hole that he avoided, because it made everything sit slightly wrong. After washing, he paired his socks and laid them out in rows, like commas.

He kept a plastic bag of old receipts, in case anyone ever asked him to prove he'd been somewhere that took money. Nobody asked. He never opened the bag. It was enough to know it was getting heavier.

One Wednesday that hadn't promised anything, the power went out for two hours. Under the moonlight the room rearranged itself into a place he didn't recognise. The edges went soft. The stain on the wall turned into a coastline at night. The chipped edge of the desk became an inlet. He sat on the floor by the window with his back against the wall. The lane's noises rose with a silver clarity, as though they had been rehearsed.

He heard his own breathing. He heard the smallest sound a person makes when he shifts his weight on tile. He said a word out loud, just to hear his voice in the changed room. It wasn't a complicated word. It was the one that had been hovering at the edge of his life for months, waiting to be invited in.

"Desolation," he said.

He didn't test synonyms. He didn't weigh the vowels for poetry. The word just sat there. It was a plain noun that didn't look at him with a dramatic eye. It named something other than the absence of people. It named the knowledge that his life had never been a place where anyone chose him. Loneliness was something late. Desolation was a platform where no train had ever been scheduled to stop.

He felt no tears. Tears need a cause. What he felt was the air thickening slowly. He leaned his head against the wall and shut his eyes. The wall was warmer than the word.

The power came back. The fan went on with its flaw. The stain became an island on plaster again. The lane's sounds dropped back to their normal volume. He got up, filled a glass of water, and drank half. He switched off the light and lay down on the cot and waited for sleep to pick him. It did, as always, out of habit rather than affection.

The next morning the milk truck came again, with the dull clink of packets going into crates. He made tea. He toasted bread on the pan until the edges burnt just enough to scrape. He left the burnt bits in the sink and washed them down later with one firm sweep of water. He put on a shirt that didn't need ironing and combed his hair, although he knew gravity would undo it by noon. He turned the key in the lock and stepped into the lane.

On the stairs he met his neighbour, an older man who liked to give advice about vegetables. "Brinjal is best on Thursdays," he said. Arun smiled, nodded, and made a mental note never to rely on this. Outside, the sun had already warmed the pavement and made it give off the day's official smell. He joined the stream heading for the metro. He stood on the platform next to a sign that warned him not to cross the yellow line, and he didn't. The train came. He got in. He held the overhead bar with his right hand and kept his left tucked in near his waist so it wouldn't brush a stranger's pocket.

He no longer thought about Meera. He thought about Kavya only when he needed to place a memory in the office timeline. He thought about Asha the way you think about a landscape once you've learned its contours and know they won't move. He allowed no small flames. He had learned that heat isn't the only way to get through a winter.

That evening he came home and stood at the window. The tea stall clinked its saucers. A woman on a balcony tucked a strand of hair behind her ear. A motorbike buzzed past with two heads turned towards each other, talking over the wind and the road and everything else you have to shout past to be heard. Arun watched them. He didn't imagine how they'd met. He didn't unspool their future. He let them be what they were for that one second. Then he looked at his reflection in the glass, faint and obedient, and pressed his fingertips to it until the prints evaporated.

He didn't move to a bigger flat. He didn't look for a noisier life. He made no resolutions. He cooked rice. He made dal that tilted thick when he was trying to correct thin. He kept the knife sharp, because blunt knives embarrass a kitchen. He threw out the coriander when it turned yellow and pretended this counted as a general kind of bravery. He changed the bedsheet on Sundays and folded the used one into a square he was pleased with, because squares don't wobble.

At night, if sleep hesitated, he counted the ways people touched in the street below. A hand on a shoulder. A child's head against a thigh. A man tapping another man's elbow to make a point. Palms around paper cups of tea that had soaked up the heat. A woman holding her own wrists while she waited for a delivery. He tallied them without envy, like birds you note on a walk. He was meticulous and cold. He cared, which isn't the same as wanting.

When the word came back to him in the morning, stripped of its moonlit dignity, he said it again anyway. Desolation. It didn't accuse anyone, and that was why he liked it. It didn't dramatise him, and that was why he trusted it. It asked for nothing. That was why it stayed.

## Part VI: The Drawer

The stationery shop was narrow and full of small noises. A bell rang when the door swung back on its spring. Pencils rattled in their tins whenever a customer brushed past. A boy at the counter rubbed a rag over a glass top that never got any brighter. Arun stood in front of the shelf of writing paper reading brand names that pretended to be European. Cream sheets, slight tooth, the kind of paper that asks for a light hand.

He bought two packets and a box of blue cartridges. The boy put them in a red carry bag that had once been pink. On the way home the bag whispered against his leg, a small paper voice insisting it was carrying something serious.

That night he cleared a space on his desk, pushing the calculator into the corner where the dust sat like tiny witnesses. He laid out a sheet. He uncapped the pen and let the nib rest on the paper a second longer than necessary. The pause felt like a ceremony.

The first letter began *Dear Unknown*. He had considered *Dear Stranger* and rejected it, since *stranger* carried an edge he didn't intend. *Unknown* was accurate without being rude.

*Dear Unknown,* he wrote. *If you have ever felt that your mouth is a room that only receives its own echo, know that somewhere a mouth is practising silence along with you.*

He didn't sign it. He stopped halfway down the second page, because he could feel the letter turning into a performance. He tore it along the crease he'd made by accident while testing the paper, and put both halves in the top right drawer.

The drawer complained when pulled. The wood had swollen in the humid months and forgotten to shrink back. Inside lay a rubber band, two paper clips that had got entangled at some point and refused to part, and old receipts gone soft from handling. He laid the letter next to them, a creature now living among evidence of purchases.

The second letter began *Dear Meera* and stopped. He tried *Dear X* and carried on for a page and a half, telling a woman who didn't exist that there was nothing to forgive because there had been no crime. He wrote about rain on a campus, and how it turns people into silhouettes that carry their own weather. He explained that a clear refusal is better than a polite lie and that he admired clarity. He folded the letter and put it in the drawer. Later he took it out and read it and found it unbearable. He didn't burn it. Burning takes an occasion. He put it back and set a receipt on top like a lid.

A letter to Kavya tried to turn into a memo. He caught himself writing bullet points, as if a rejection could be set out as an agenda. He stopped and wrote one sentence instead. *Your snacks were always spicier than I let on, and I pretended they weren't because I wanted to belong inside your small circle round a flask.* He looked at it for a long time, folded the paper in four, and tucked it under the others.

The letter to Asha was the easiest to write and the hardest to keep. *Dear Asha, thank you for reaching for my hand and then putting it back on the table so carefully.* He wrote that kindness is a blanket that can't be tucked in at the corners. He wrote about the steel counter in the café showing two faces like a prophecy. He wrote that he had stopped wanting, and that he wasn't angry about it. He folded it and put it away. Two days later he took it out and read it and felt his face go hot for no clear reason. He slid it back and pushed the drawer shut harder than usual, as if that would nail the letter to the wood.

He wrote one to his father too, and it was the shortest. *Dear Achan, the pressure cooker is fine. I am fine. Please show the knee to a doctor.* He read it back and saw that it was the only letter in the drawer that could have been posted without anyone being embarrassed. He put it in with the others anyway.

On Sunday he walked to the post office to buy stamps, because you can't write letters without imagining an exit. The post office smelled of glue and slow time. The man at the counter pressed a stamp onto the corner of a parcel with his thumb, a gesture he'd made a thousand times without ever learning to be bored by it. The clerk sold Arun a strip of stamps with a small bird printed in a red that leaned towards tired. Arun rolled the strip up, put a rubber band round it, and put it in the drawer as well.

He never addressed an envelope. He never wrote a name in the top left corner. He folded the sheets and stacked them, cream on cream, as if keeping them level would stop his thoughts climbing out when he wasn't looking. The drawer started to feel like a lung. He opened and closed it without using it. When he left for work in the morning the desk looked ordinary: a calculator, a lamp whose bulb was slightly yellower than the room needed, and a drawer full of letters that had decided to stay citizens of a closed space.

On the bad nights, when sleep wouldn't come and the room felt heavy, he sat at the desk and wrote fragments. *Dear Unknown, I watched a couple in the lane share one cup of tea, and I didn't picture them as grateful.* He crossed out *grateful* and wrote *lucky*, crossed that out too, and wrote nothing. On another sheet: *Dear Future Me, I'm not here to warn you off anything. I only want to tell you it can be done, this carrying on.*

He wrote a letter to himself at eighty once. Somewhere he had read that people get old by accident more than by plan. *Dear Arun,* he wrote, *at eighty, I suppose, the face stops haggling with life and takes the terms.* He asked the old man whether not being chosen had changed shape up there. He asked whether *desolation* had got heavier or lighter. He wrote the questions and left the answers blank, to keep a place where the silence could belong to itself.

Some Sundays he read the whole drawer in order, from the first torn page to the latest. He noticed that the letters had got better written and less honest. The early ones stumbled and said things straight. The later ones had good sentences, balanced and clean, and he could tell that somewhere along the way he had started writing for a reader. He wasn't sure who. He wasn't sure he minded.

By the third month the drawer had weight. When he slid it open the papers moved together, like one body. He thought about sealing them in envelopes and addressing them to no one. He thought about writing a single address and putting all the letters inside one larger envelope, like a family. He did neither. He left the drawer as it was, and began to think of it as a quiet machine that turned evenings into something you could hold.

Once, late at night, he took a letter out and walked to the blue postbox by the temple. His hands shook in a way that embarrassed him, because his hands had never been the dramatic part of him. He stood with the slot open and the letter against his palm. A boy on a bicycle went past, singing the one line of a song worth remembering. A woman with a shopping bag hoisted the bag higher on her shoulder and looked at the sky as if it had just said something foolish. Arun held the letter at the point where a person pushes it in and lets go. He stood like that for three breaths, then four. He took the letter back home and put it in the drawer. He slept without dreams and woke up annoyed with his body for being so practical.

The letters kept multiplying, though not the way wanting does. They spread like moss, slow and quiet, always new and always the same. He was learning that writing wasn't a way out. It was a way of lining the inside of the room, so the air in it wouldn't come apart.

## Part VII: Commute, City, Theatres of Nearness

The morning metro was a piece of choreography that pretended to be an accident. He stood between a man frowning at his messages and a woman holding a lunchbox with her wrist turned inward to protect it from the crowd. By his knee a child in school uniform swayed back and forth, counting the stations backwards because it made them pass faster. The train slid into tunnels and out into light. Phone signals dropped and came back. Everyone looked up in the same second when a message buzzed through. The carriage was a mouthful of breaths timed to the track.

At Kaloor two lovers got in, still at the stage where people repeat the names of ordinary objects as if they've just discovered language. Wallet. Ticket. Platform. One of them laughed into a hand and the other tried to keep a straight face and failed. They stood by the door, too close to be strangers and not close enough to be alone. At each station their bodies readjusted to keep the same warmth between them. Arun watched the way you watch a demonstration of a principle you remember from school but can't state exactly.

On days when the morning felt like a stone, he got off one station early. The extra walk gave him a lever inside the day. The road from that station went past a park where old men stretched their thighs and discussed government decisions that could be improved with a little common sense. Dogs lay with one ear up in case news arrived. Couples used the far path, which curved behind a hedge before coming back into the open. Arun took the middle path. He watched how one person's shoulder slips into the space another person's body leaves for it, and reminded himself that this wasn't a metaphor he needed to extend.

At the tea stall near his stop he had become a regular, which in practice meant that the man made his tea without asking and slid it across without looking up. Nobody knew his name. "Ginger chaya," the man once said to a new boy, nodding in Arun's direction as the entire explanation. It was the closest thing to being known that he had, and he took it home with him and kept it, like a coin.

Sometimes he had breakfast at a stall. Idlis steaming in a cloth bundle. Sambar, thin and bright. The man behind the counter split vadas open at a speed that was almost musical. The women serving water did it without looking up from their thoughts. The table was wiped, and wiped again, not because it was still dirty but because wiping is a substitute for doing something permanent. A man and a woman sat two stools away and argued about rent as if rent were a decision made by a friend who would listen if only addressed correctly. It wasn't a bitter argument. It had the rhythm of two people who could afford to exaggerate for effect. When the vadas arrived they stopped talking and ate with the urgency of people who enjoy the part of life that would survive even if words didn't. The woman wiped a dot of sambar off the man's lip with her thumb and then looked at her thumb as if it were a new object. He kissed it quickly. Arun paid and left an extra five rupees, a tax for having witnessed something.

On weekends he walked down to the river. Water makes you try to interpret it, and then it makes your interpretation look like a cup dipped into the sea. People came to watch it take its time. A wedding photographer was arranging a couple under a tree, the bride balanced on a root, the groom trying to look brave in shoes that weren't built for mud. Children flung bread at fish that didn't want it. A man carried his mother's handbag because her shoulder had given up on it. Teenagers in matching jeans took photographs with their heads tilted the same way. Arun stood at the railing and gripped it with both hands when the ground seemed to lean. It leaned because he had caught himself hoping he might end up in somebody's photograph by accident. He switched the thought off, the way you turn off a tap that's been dripping.

In the late evenings the city undid its top button. Balconies became stages. People watered their plants, and the water hitting the railings made a sound like a small rain in rehearsal. Radios played songs that had lived through too many summers to be hurt any more. In the lane below his window a woman waited for a man on a bike, and finally sat down with her back to the wall and her eyes closed. He arrived late. She stood up without a word, climbed on behind him, and laid her palm flat on his spine, the way you close a book. The bike's noise trailed away and folded back into the lane. Arun had been standing at the window so long that he began to suspect it was what he actually did for a living.

Festivals arrived on schedule. Lights strung between houses became a kind of public courage. A procession went by with a deity decked in colours that expected applause. Drums argued with chests. Firecrackers rehearsed their small quarrels with the sky. Arun stood in the crowd, because crowds let you feel alone more honestly. He kept his arms at his sides and let the music walk through him without taking anything he couldn't afford to lose. A child on his left grabbed hold of his trouser pocket by mistake, believing him to be a father's shape. The mistake lasted two seconds. The child let go like a leaf.

He went to the cinema when the quiet of the flat got too much. The hall was darkness arranged in rows. Popcorn smell. Armrests slightly damp, and not asking for your confidence. On screen people found each other because the story required it. Their lines had already been written, so desire looked like fate. A man two seats away whispered into his partner's hair. A phone lit up three rows behind and made a bright rectangle that nagged at the dark. The audience gasped on cue at a twist nobody needed. Arun watched and allowed himself the discipline of going back to the lane alone afterwards, with no story to explain why. It felt correct to come out into the night without any additional fiction.

Sometimes in the metro a person set a bag down where he'd been standing a second earlier and said sorry without meeting his eye. Sometimes he caught his own reflection in the window and had to look away, because a reflection is only useful when it confirms something. Sometimes a child leaned his head on Arun's elbow because a moving train turns your body into furniture. He kept his elbow steady. The mother mouthed a thank you.

He saw a couple break up once on a bench outside a pharmacy. The woman's shoulders came up as if to protect her neck. The man kept shaking his head at a sum that wouldn't change however many times he shook it. They didn't cry. They used sentences that sounded like receipts. Arun thought it was unfair that he was there to see it, and then understood that fairness isn't something benches can tell you. He walked home carefully, and that night he took out a sheet of paper and wrote: *Dear Unknown, I saw an ending today that didn't need a witness.*

## Part VIII: Work, the Small Machinery of Other People

The office went on with its calendar of invented events. HR sent round an email asking everyone to wear blue for Harmony Day. People wore blue and then spent fifteen minutes comparing their blue to somebody else's blue. Photographs were taken in front of a banner printed with a quote from someone whose name had been misspelled. Arun stood at the edge of the group, so that if anyone needed to crop the picture for the newsletter, taking him out wouldn't injure the frame.

Birthdays at work were held around a cake with a plastic knife that gleamed as if every previous birthday had polished it. People clapped before the person had blown out the candles, because clapping handles embarrassment. The candles were the kind that resist and then give in dramatically. Arun said "Happy birthday" and watched a piece of cake travel towards him on a paper plate. He accepted it and ate as little icing as politeness allowed. Someone shouted, "Speech!" The birthday person said they were grateful to be part of this family. The word *family* floated over the cubicles for a while and settled on a dust mote.

Performance reviews came in a tone that was neither excited nor critical. His manager said "solid hand" and wrote *reliable* on a line where people usually put *passionate* and *driven*. Arun nodded and agreed to improve his stakeholder communication, which is what managers say when they haven't had to correct your work. He said thank you at the right time and left the room. He didn't update his résumé, because he had learned that some acts begin a draft of a life you may not want to submit.

A new hire joined the team and called him *bhaiya* in the first week. Arun laughed softly and told him to just use his name. The young man tried twice, failed, and went back to *bhaiya*, because a language keeps its old furniture even when it buys new chairs. He fell in love with someone from another department and told Arun about it, with the kind of pride that looks like fear trained to stand up straight. Arun listened and said all the sentences that make a person sound like a supporter of human progress. He was a supporter. He said "good for you" and meant it. He said it well.

December belonged to Secret Santa, which made it easier to get through. Arun bought a small pot of basil for a colleague who hadn't known she wanted a smell to grow indoors. He got a mug with a slogan about coffee, printed with no real belief. When the gifts were opened the room sounded like traffic stopping at a zebra crossing. A man grinned at a pair of headphones. A woman acted as though she loved the book she'd been given. Arun sipped water from his mug, which told him coffee was life, and wondered why mugs always try so hard to speak for mouths.

Four people left in a single year, each with a farewell speech. Better pay, a shorter commute, or no reason given. They stood by the pantry and called the company a great learning experience. One of them told the story of a night spent hunting a bug that turned out to be the wrong bug, and everybody laughed. Cake, photographs, again. Arun stood at the back with people who wouldn't remember him being there when they came across the picture six months on. He didn't resent it. Memory has a budget like everything else, and he was a small line in it.

He walked back to his desk past a glass wall that reflected him with a clerk's accuracy. In the glass his face looked like the face that lift companies put in their brochures to show how much room there is. Behind him people were saying *sprint*. He asked himself whether he needed a story that explained him to himself. He decided no, not because he didn't want one, but because wanting had learned to keep quiet.

At lunch he sat with two colleagues who liked to speculate about an office romance between two others. They used the words *chemistry* and *vibe* in a way that made the air smell faintly of eucalyptus. He ate and nodded. One of them asked if he had noticed how the two sat closer now. He said he hadn't noticed, and knew at once that this made him sound like a liar. The conversation went on without needing him.

On a Tuesday his manager asked him to run a small training for the interns on data hygiene. The word *hygiene* gave him a precise pleasure. He prepared slides nobody would remember and spoke clearly enough to persuade the interns that they were being given a chance to become meticulous. One of them stayed back afterwards to ask about a shortcut. She said "Thank you, sir" with a respect that wasn't flattering. He went back to his desk and changed the titles on his slides so they matched the format of the other training materials. Nobody had asked. He did it anyway, because the world improves in centimetres.

There were days when a colleague hugged someone in the corridor and the hug lasted three seconds longer than usual. There were days when an email arrived with an attachment called *Proposal_Final_2* and everyone pretended to believe the name. There were days when he ate lunch alone because of back-to-back meetings and was surprised at how nicely solitude behaves when it doesn't have to compete. There were days when laughter burst out from a group behind him and his body got ready to laugh along and then remembered it hadn't been asked. He let the laughter pass through him like wind through a bamboo blind.

One evening a woman in the next row put her head down on her desk for a minute. Nobody asked if she was fine, because everyone knew better than to ask. After a minute she sat up and went back to typing, as if the keyboard had called her by her childhood name. Arun thought it was admirable. You can be dignified without making a scene.

In the lift a man he barely knew asked if he was married. He said no. The man nodded and then, searching for a phrase that wouldn't offend God or luck, said, "It will happen," and got off two floors early. Arun stayed and watched the doors close, counting the floors. He didn't correct the man. There are belief systems you don't take apart out of courtesy.

## Part IX: The Desert and the Questions That Refuse Answers

At night the flat set its facts out around him. The fan stumbled, as it did. The stain held its shoreline. The drawer held its letters, addressed to nobody, to everybody, to versions of himself he might never meet. He had stopped confusing looking back with looking ahead. What he had learned was simpler: you carry on, and carrying on is a verb in its own right.

When sleep didn't arrive on time, he let his mind try the thoughts that daytime doesn't permit because they take up too much room. He let them move slowly, like animals that startle easily.

He began with an observation he had earned by living. Human beings are partly made by the eyes of other human beings. We learn the outline of ourselves from the look of welcome, from the surface of someone else's hunger, from the small repairs lovers make in us when they say what they want. Nobody had met him that way. He had been assessed, appreciated for being reliable, sympathised with, and refused: efficiently, irritably, pityingly. None of those looks is the look that confirms a body as wanted.

He didn't blame anyone. He could have made a list of failures, but lists are poor instruments for music. He tried to ask a cleaner question instead. If a person is never desired, what changes in him? It was a question about the body and about something beyond it. He could feel the answer, but he couldn't say it.

He tried images. A violin left on a shelf is still a violin. It can be tuned, dusted, insured. It doesn't turn into a chair because nobody plays it. It stays an instrument in a desert. But wood warps, and strings slacken, and the room forgets that sound needs a bow. The instrument becomes honest about its silence. He held on to this for a minute and let it go, because an instrument always has a hand somewhere in the world waiting for it. People don't.

He thought about history. People are remembered for the wars they won, the bridges they built, the loves they made others witness. Great romances produce smaller ones. Letters end up in museums. Photographs in private albums outlast their subjects. He could picture a future in which a landlord went through his papers because he needed the drawer for the next tenant. A stamp with a bird on it would fall to the floor and be swept into a bag. He wasn't afraid of that. He was almost relieved at how accurate it was.

He thought about biology. Desire is a current that asks the body to come forward. He had felt that current, and then felt it slow down until it was a lake with no streams. He didn't moralise about this. He wasn't a monk who wanted sainthood. He was a citizen of a quiet country with a passport that expired on time. He wondered sometimes whether bodies that don't receive certain looks find another circuit for the excess. He noticed he cooked more carefully once the weather cooled. He noticed he cleaned the windows with more attention after festival days. He noticed he took longer to square the corners of the bedsheet. He used care where other people use ardour. He didn't call that a victory. He called it substitution.

He thought about the word *unwanted* and found it too loud. It suggested a decision made by a committee. He didn't believe anyone had met to vote on him. He preferred *unchosen*. It accounted for the absence without inventing malice. It left room for the fact that everyone lives inside a distribution they didn't design. He hadn't been chosen for games, for notes, for names said softly, for the brief madness that couples attempt and sometimes survive. He had been chosen for tasks, for laughter in groups, for competence under irritation. These aren't nothing. He respected them.

He went back to the platform image. Loneliness as a delay. Desolation as the timetable that never had your train in it. He found comfort in the difference because it stopped him from bargaining. Bargaining wears down dignity. He wanted to keep his.

Sometimes his thoughts turned sharp and asked whether he had been a coward. He gave the question its place in the room. He had confessed three times. He had been refused three times, in three different flavours: puzzled, irritated, gentle. After the third, wanting had started to look ridiculous, so he'd stopped. If that was cowardice, he could live with it. Courage sometimes looks like setting a glass down gently on a table instead of smashing it against the wall to prove walls exist.

He wondered whether other people like him were scattered across the city, each performing a life with the right gestures, each doing the small hygienic acts that keep a country from sticking together. He liked to imagine them, because it gave him a sense of brotherhood that didn't require meeting anyone. Once he wrote to them on a sheet. *Dear Others Who Will Not Be Named, we are not a movement. We are a mineral. We exist and add weight to the earth.*

There was one more question he allowed himself, as a treat. A man who is never desired: is he free of some fears, or trapped in others? He had no answer. He lay there listening to the fan find its flaw, lose it, and find it again. The repetition was oddly restful. If the fan could keep going, so could he.

He thought about the baby he'd seen at the tea stall, laughing at a spoon that had caught the light. He thought about the old woman on the ground floor who sat out every afternoon with her feet in a basin of warm water, going cloudy with relief. He thought about the stray dog that knew exactly when a certain man would drop half a biscuit. All of them proved the world wasn't mocking him. All of them also proved it could be sweet without him. He found he could hold both at once, and neither argued with the other.

He thought about the letters. They weren't a cry for help, he told himself. They were a record. Someone might read them one day, after the room had been cleared, and think: this man took himself seriously enough to write it down. He smiled at his own vanity and let it be. It wasn't hurting anyone.

He went back to the word he had said in the power cut. Desolation. He said it now without any moonlight. It hadn't lost its shape. It didn't dramatise him. It named a landscape and let him walk across it without pretending he saw water where there wasn't any.

## Part X: Ending Without End

Morning came back together the way it always did, slowly and without fuss. The truck dropped the milk at the shop with its usual clink. The lane filled with scooters that deserved more attention than they got. Somewhere a woman called for a child, who ignored her, and then came. A vegetable seller sang out his list in a tune the whole lane knew.

Arun got up and folded the sheet. He washed his cup and left it to dry at an angle where it wouldn't hold a drop. He looked at the window and decided it was clean enough to ignore for two more days. He opened the drawer, laid a fresh sheet of paper inside without writing on it, and closed it with his palm flat against the wood, feeling it take the pressure.

He dressed for work in the shirt that needed no argument. He combed his hair. He left the flat, locked the door, and put the key in his pocket, where it would press a small square into his thigh if he sat too long in one place.

He walked to the metro with everyone else. On the platform he stood behind the yellow line and watched a sparrow hop about in a gap where nobody had noticed that birds had decided to live. The train came, the doors opened, he got in, held the overhead bar with his right hand and kept his left close to his waist. At a station where the light fell in properly, he saw his reflection in the dark window and didn't look away. The face looking back had the calm that you can only buy with repeated days.

At lunch he ate alone and enjoyed it without needing to defend solitude as a philosophy. In the afternoon he changed a date format in a sheet because consistency comforted him. In the evening he got off one station early and took the path through the park and counted six dogs, three couples, and two men practising a speech for an event that would soon require it. He reached home, turned the key, went in, and the room received him with its practised silence.

He put water on to boil. He cut a potato into equal pieces and felt the small satisfaction that comes only when things obey. He salted the dal at the exact moment before it could accuse him of being late. He ate with the discipline of someone who has learned that attention is a food group. He washed the plate and put it on the rack. He wiped the desk with a cloth and watched a dark line on the wood brighten and then go back to its old colour, because wood remembers.

He stood at the window and watched the lane become itself. The tea stall clinked its cups. The television sent out cheers at the wrong moment. A motorbike came up, two people on it, the second one's palm resting on the first one's side. A woman on a balcony watered a plant that didn't need permission to keep living. A child laughed again at a sound that wouldn't have made an adult laugh. The light went, the way it always does, without asking anyone.

He put both hands on the sill and leaned forward a little. He didn't pray. He didn't wish. He didn't compose a letter in his head. He stood and watched until watching turned into a kind of sleep.

There was no late confession waiting for him. No miraculous meeting of eyes. No twist. He had reached out and been refused, and then he had accepted the arithmetic that followed. He had carried on. He would carry on. Habit would carry him when desire couldn't. That didn't make him noble. It made him present.

Above him the fan stumbled on its fourth beat and caught itself, as it always did. The stain on the wall kept its shoreline. The drawer kept its papers. The city went loud, then quiet. He stayed where he was until his fingerprints faded off the sill. That was his life, as far as he could put it into words tonight. It wasn't an argument and it wasn't an apology. It was just how things stood. 

He switched off the light. The room went on being a room, and he went on being in it. It was hollow. But it was his. And he’d never wanted anything more than that.
