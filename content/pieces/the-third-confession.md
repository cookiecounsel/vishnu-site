At thirty-one he felt like a man wheeling a bicycle through a lane with traffic in both directions, fast enough to make decisions complicated and slow enough to make hurrying look silly. He wasn't defeated. He wouldn't have dramatised it if he were. He was practical, careful, employed. He rang his parents every other week and sent his mother a link to a cheaper pressure cooker. He fixed a leaking tap himself. He switched tea brands when the shopkeeper told him the new one was fresher. All of this counted as a life.

Asha arrived in it without any ceremony. She worked in another vertical and came into his orbit through a shared project, then stayed at its edge after the project ended. They found out they liked the same old film for different reasons. She loved the way the heroine held a silence; he loved the scene where the hero walks across a field and there is no music. She ate quickly and quietly and finished her plate before she finished her sentence. She listened with her chin on her fist, which made her look more serious than the subject needed. When she laughed at one of his small jokes he felt warm, and the warmth didn't make him stupid. It made him tidy.

They started having lunch together when the tables weren't full. They never agreed to it. If she was there he sat across from her; if she wasn't, he ate alone. He didn't plan his day around hers. He respected the discretion of chance.

She asked questions that felt like presents you could actually hold. "What did you read as a child," she asked once, "and which of those books would you avoid rereading, so they don't shrink?" Nobody had ever asked him that. He told her about a book of stories from the library near his house, the blue stamp inside the cover, the smell a book gets when many hands have turned it slowly. He said he wouldn't reread it. He wanted it to stay the size it was. She nodded as if he'd said something that made sense.

In the second month she said, "Shall we try that café on the corner? The one that thinks it's French." They went after work. The café had yellow bulbs in little glass cages. They sat by the window with two cups of tea in mugs with long handles that made you hold them wrong. She told him about a cousin who refused to wear shoes at a wedding because they pinched his toes. He told her about the lizard behind his fridge that came out only when he left a slice of banana, to test whether lizards eat fruit. They don't, in his experience.

After that the café became theirs, in the loose way things were ever theirs. She argued with the counter boy about the price of a croissant that wasn't one. He learned that she took her tea without sugar and then stole his biscuit. Sometimes they sat for an hour and said almost nothing, and he found that this didn't frighten him. He kept waiting for the old tightness in his chest, the feeling of being a guest in a stranger's house, and it kept not arriving.

It occurred to him that affection didn't have to be a room you rushed into. It could be a chair you kept coming back to because it fitted your back better than the others. He wasn't a boy talking to a mirror any more. He was a man who could name what he wanted without swallowing the person in front of him. He was quietly proud of that.

The day he decided to say it was an ordinary Tuesday. Ordinary is the best container for a decision that might fail. They went to the small café near the office, since the big one had been chaos all week. The boy at the counter knew Asha's order and smiled at her. The steel counter reflected their faces as thin streaks. They sat at a table against the wall, under a fake chalkboard listing the specials in English, with the confidence of someone who has never been corrected in public.

He could have waited. He could have let another month of conversations pile up like plates. But he had learned that patience can turn into an apology for fear, and that fear sets traps in whatever time you give it. He didn't want to be trapped. He also didn't want to be ridiculous.

"Asha," he said as they put their cups down, "I need to tell you something."

She looked up with the patient attention that made people say more than they'd planned. He could see the two of them in the steel behind the counter, faint and small.

"I like you," he said. "More than as a friend."

The silence that followed took real time. He watched it fill the table. He had prepared himself to be refused. He hadn't prepared for how gently a refusal could be arranged.

She reached across and put her hand over his. It was light and warm and careful. It reminded him of a towel pressed on a bruise.

"Arun," she said, "I'm sorry. I care about you. I do. But not like that. You deserve someone who sees you that way, and I don't."

He nodded as if she'd given him directions to a place he had no plans to visit. "I understand," he said, because understanding was what he had always offered whenever he was asked for anything. He didn't take his hand away until she did. He lifted his mug and burnt his tongue, because the tea was hotter than he'd expected. He smiled. She smiled. They talked about a problem at work the way you talk about a neighbour's dog that has run into your house. The conversation stayed calm. The problem wasn't his or hers. It was something someone else could fix.

On the walk back to the office he let her do the talking. He noticed that his legs had picked a pace which made her take a half-step quicker than usual, so he slowed down. She asked him about something that used to interest him and he answered like a man reading an old signboard.

That night he sat on the edge of his cot and counted the loudest sounds in his room. The fan. A horn outside. A neighbour dragging a bucket across the bathroom floor. He tried to give each sound exactly one thought, so that the thoughts would stay countable. They didn't. Thoughts multiply like mould once you let moisture in, and he had let it in.

The week after that, they had lunch twice, then once, then not at all. Nobody was punishing anybody. It was only the balance sorting itself out. When they did cross paths they smiled and traded small sentences, light enough to hold in the mouth. He felt grateful, and oddly relieved. Kindness had done the cruellest thing it can. It had taken away his right to be angry.

On Sunday he went to the market and bought a packet of cream-coloured writing paper. He told himself it was for a letter to an aunt. He didn't write to the aunt. He opened the packet and ran his fingers over the top sheet. It had faint ruled lines you could only see at a certain angle. They felt like rules you accept because you're tired of improvising.
