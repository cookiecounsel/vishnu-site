Adulthood turned out to be a beige room with a lot of computers humming in it. At twenty-seven Arun was an analyst, a title that suggested rigour and hid a great deal of copy-paste. He had a spreadsheet open, a report template saved under a name he never changed, and a reputation for being dependable. People asked him for numbers when they needed to impress someone else, and he gave them numbers, and the numbers behaved.

The office smelled of air-conditioning and instant coffee. The corridor carpet had a pattern designed to hide stains, which it did very well. Three times a week the snack trolley came round at four with a clatter of cups and a spoon banging against a flask, and people stood up from their cubicles as if someone had called their names. Jokes made the rounds the same way. You could hear one at the far end of the floor, slightly changed, ten minutes after you'd heard it near the pantry. Arun laughed in the right places. He kept a few sugar sachets lined up on his desk, on the theory that it gave him a personality.

Kavya sat in the next row of cubicles. She wasn't someone who made an entrance. She simply made the spot where she stood a little warmer. Her hair clip never matched her earrings. She brought snacks in zip-lock pouches and offered them to whoever was within reach. She asked ordinary questions, and people answered with far more detail than the questions had asked for. "How was your weekend?" and someone would end up showing her photographs of a child at a fountain. "Did you watch that new series?" and a man would re-enact a whole scene in the corridor. One afternoon she held the pouch out to Arun. "Try. It's spicy, careful." A mix of sev and peanuts and dried peas.

The spice was a small ceremony. He coughed once, for form's sake, and she laughed. It wasn't Meera's laugh. It was a practical one, the kind that tells you the room is still in one piece.

In the first month he thanked her for the snacks and went back to his spreadsheet, a little ashamed of himself. In the second month his attention began to drift. He caught himself waiting for four o'clock. He built small arrangements of peanuts at the corner of his desk, a triangle, then a square. He started asking about her weekends in the careful way of a man learning a language late in life. She told him about a cousin's engagement and a market where lilies were sold tied up with kitchen twine. He listened the way you listen when you're hoping there will be room in the story for you.

He didn't confuse friendliness with something else. He told himself that regularly, like a drill. People were touchy about being misread, and he knew it. But when her sleeve brushed his arm as she pointed to a cell on his screen, his heart jumped and then acted as if nothing had happened. He learned when she took her tea breaks and found reasons to be near the pantry at the same time. He rehearsed again, but these weren't confessions. They were smaller things, ways of asking for a little of her time. "There's a new café near the metro, want to try?" "I've been curious about that vegetarian place." He tried to imagine the question in different corridors, so he could pick the one where it would sound least like trespassing.

One evening at the end of the quarter, the office emptied early because people with families were running for trains that wouldn't wait. The light went grey and soft and made the carpet look less aggressive. Kavya was squinting at a chart that would be changed again before morning. Arun stood up, sat down, stood up again, and reminded himself that nothing happens when you do nothing. Then he walked to the edge of her cubicle.

"Kavya."

She looked up with a neutral smile that could have turned into anything. He held on to the partition because his hands wanted a job.

"I was thinking," he said, "if you'd like to go out sometime. Dinner, maybe."

The moment gathered into one dense point, and everything had to pass through it. He watched her face reorganise itself. The friendliness didn't disappear. It stepped back one pace.

"Arun," she said, and his name already sounded like the first line of a policy. "Don't misunderstand. I'm just being friendly. Please don't make this awkward."

She turned back to her screen and tapped out three firm keystrokes that didn't mean anything. The air between them had changed, and neither of them did anything about it.

He stayed there long enough for it to look silly. "Of course," he said, though he wasn't sure what he was agreeing to, and walked back to his desk slowly, as if slow would make less noise.

The next day the pouch did not come across the corridor. She didn't look over her monitor to ask how he'd slept. She talked to the others about a TV serial, in the same practical laugh, which was no longer meant for him. He went to refill his water bottle twice and came back with it half full both times, because some errands have to be made to look longer than they are.

Praveen from procurement, who had opinions on everything, found him at the water cooler that week. "Don't take it personally," he said. "She's nice to everyone. It's practically in her appraisal." Arun said he wasn't taking it personally. "Good, good," said Praveen, who could clearly tell he was, and went off to tell somebody else.

At lunch Arun ate faster than usual and felt overfull. He told himself he hadn't been humiliated. He had been corrected. He thought about the word *awkward* and admired it. It pointed at a discomfort that had nothing to do with pain, and it kept everything on the surface and made the surface slippery.

Over the weekend he tried to reset himself. He ironed his shirts and lined them up by shade, lightest to darkest. He cleaned the one shelf where he kept his books. He went to the market and bought coriander, because coriander brightens a kitchen even when it wilts. He watched a cricket match and let the commentary do his thinking. During the ad break he muted the TV and listened to the ceiling. The silence inside the mute was different from the silence of no sound. It had the weight of something taken away.

On Monday he went back to the role, which he had learned well. The office, the corridor, the trolley, the sugar sachets. He gave Kavya a nod with exactly the right dose of neutral. A colleague showed her a photograph of a child at a fountain, again, and Arun looked at it and said "Cute," in the tone he'd borrowed from a hundred earlier approvals. The child had an orange-stained mouth. He wondered what snack had done it, and whether anyone had told the child he was adorable at that moment, or whether the adults had waited until there was a camera.

He didn't write any of this in a notebook. He didn't tell anyone that his heart had lurched once and then fixed its posture. The office had swallowed bigger sorrows than his without a mark on the carpet. He took the lesson it was offering. Be legible. Don't take up more air than you need.
