Arun was never really noticed, and it began so early that he could not have said when. Nobody did anything to him. That was the trouble. If somebody had, there would have been something to point at.

In Class Four the boys chose sides for cricket every afternoon. Two captains, two lines, the same ritual each day. The good ones went in the first minute: the fast bowler who grinned at everything, the boy who could hit the ball over the compound wall, the loud one who argued with every decision and still scored when it mattered. Then the middle went. Then four or five of them were left standing around, pretending they didn't mind. Arun was usually among them. Somebody would count heads, sigh, and say, "Okay, Arun, you come this side." Nobody was cruel about it. Nobody was happy about it either.

He learned to follow a game by its sound. The shouting rose a little whenever something was about to happen, and it almost never rose for him. One day he got to bat. The bowler lobbed the ball with a smirk and the fielders drifted about lazily. Arun hit it cleanly. He felt the thump run up through his wrists and watched the ball roll past a fielder who was looking at something else. Then he looked up and waited for someone to cheer. Two boys near the boundary were arguing about last week's match. The bowler asked whether that counted as a hit. "Play on," said the captain, bored already.

Around that time the class teacher asked everyone to bring a family photograph for the noticeboard. By the next morning the board was full of weddings, cousins, grandmothers in shawls, birthday cakes, brothers in cricket whites. Arun brought a small matte photo from a studio near the bus stand. His parents stood on either side of him with a hand on each of his shoulders, all three of them stiff in front of a painted backdrop of clouds and a rainbow. The teacher pinned the photographs up in rows, with a blue ribbon running along the top. His ended up in the bottom corner, slightly crooked, just past the point where the ribbon stopped. He straightened the pin after school, when the room was empty.

Birthdays at home were small. His mother bought a round cake with a pink border and one plastic rose in the middle. The lighter never worked on the first click. His father hummed a film song while he cut paper plates out of old newspaper. One year Arun invited five boys from his class. Two were going out of station. One's mother didn't allow him out in the evenings. One had tuition. One said he would come and didn't. "It's raining," his mother said, and put her hand on his hair, as if the rain explained everything. In the end the cake was cut with cousins. He smiled for the photographs. It was a small cake, and it was still too much cake.

He turned out to be good at other people's birthdays. He remembered every one of them, and every year he wrote the message before anyone else did, with the right number of exclamation marks. The replies came back quickly: "Thanks da!" with a smiley. By evening his message was buried under forty others, and he didn't mind, because that was how it worked. You were a name on a list that people were glad to see once a year.

In Class Seven the teacher stopped at his bench during roll call, frowned at her chart, and asked the room, "Who sits here again?" Everyone laughed, the easy laugh of a class that has found a moment that doesn't matter. Somebody told her his name. She nodded and moved on. For days afterwards the question kept coming back to him at odd times. Who sits here again. It didn't hurt exactly. It made him feel thinner, as if his outline was a pencil drawing someone had started to rub out.

When he was twelve there was a class picnic. He took the bus across town with a bottle of water sweating in his bag and a list of things his mother had made him check twice. At the park everyone ran towards a fountain shaped like a fish. He waited for someone to call him, and nobody did, so he walked there alone and stopped once to shake a stone out of his shoe. For the scavenger hunt the teacher told them to form groups of five. By the time a boy waved him over, the groups had already counted themselves. "Sorry, full," the boy said, and went back to talking about a clue shaped like a leaf. The teacher made a new group out of whoever was left. They hadn't been picked. They had been placed. There is a difference, and he noticed it every time.

At the annual day in Class Nine he was given the job of pulling the curtain. It was an important job, the teacher said, and he believed her until the play began. For forty minutes he stood in the wings with a rope in his hands and watched the others take their bows. When the curtain finally closed on the last scene, he was the only person backstage with nothing to take off, no costume, no make-up, nobody to hug. His mother said later that she could see him from the third row and that he'd done it beautifully. He thanked her. It was the most anyone had said about it.

Growing up didn't fix anything. He watched the others move into the language of being young, and he watched like a visitor who can follow the grammar but can't speak. The boys whispered about girls and ribbed each other and respected each other in the same breath. The girls laughed and shoved each other's shoulders. Folded notes went around inside textbooks, and hands touched a little longer than a joke needed. Arun laughed when others laughed. He copied expressions carefully, the way a student copies a diagram off the board. He wasn't an outcast and he wasn't in. He stood where the floor continues past the edge of the carpet.

He started a small notebook of things other people seemed to know without being taught. What to talk about at break. When to cut into a conversation without breaking its rhythm. Questions that sound interested without sounding hungry. He practised saying "How was your test?" and then leaving a pause for the answer to turn into a conversation. The answers never did. They dropped like biscuit crumbs and stayed where they fell.

At fifteen he watched a film alone for the first time. He told his parents it was a class outing. He stood in the ticket queue between two boys complaining about the hero's new hairstyle and a group of girls comparing earrings. The man at the counter tore his ticket without looking up. Inside, the back rows were full of couples, and the laughter in there sounded like bees. When the lights came on, the hall emptied in two directions. Arun stayed in his seat a few seconds longer than he needed to. It wasn't sadness. It was closer to taking notes.

At seventeen a girl from his class asked him for a favour. "Can you stand in the corridor and shout if Ma'am comes? I need to return a book and I don't want her to know I read it during maths." She smiled the way people smile when they are sure you won't complicate their day. He said yes. He stood there watching the corridor and the girl at once, hoping for a second request that would carry some warmth. The teacher turned the corner. He called out. The girl dashed back, thanked him while already moving past, and handed the book over with a perfect smile. He had been a hinge. Doors swung on him.

By the time school ended, the facts of his life had set into something simple. People liked him. People didn't choose him. The two were not the same, and he knew exactly where the line ran. He took it with him to college, the way you carry a scar on your back: you know it's there, but you can't see it without a mirror. He didn't resent anyone. He wasn't built for resentment. He was built for putting up with things.
