The morning metro was a piece of choreography that pretended to be an accident. He stood between a man frowning at his messages and a woman holding a lunchbox with her wrist turned inward to protect it from the crowd. By his knee a child in school uniform swayed back and forth, counting the stations backwards because it made them pass faster. The train slid into tunnels and out into light. Phone signals dropped and came back. Everyone looked up in the same second when a message buzzed through. The carriage was a mouthful of breaths timed to the track.

At Kaloor two lovers got in, still at the stage where people repeat the names of ordinary objects as if they've just discovered language. Wallet. Ticket. Platform. One of them laughed into a hand and the other tried to keep a straight face and failed. They stood by the door, too close to be strangers and not close enough to be alone. At each station their bodies readjusted to keep the same warmth between them. Arun watched the way you watch a demonstration of a principle you remember from school but can't state exactly.

On days when the morning felt like a stone, he got off one station early. The extra walk gave him a lever inside the day. The road from that station went past a park where old men stretched their thighs and discussed government decisions that could be improved with a little common sense. Dogs lay with one ear up in case news arrived. Couples used the far path, which curved behind a hedge before coming back into the open. Arun took the middle path. He watched how one person's shoulder slips into the space another person's body leaves for it, and reminded himself that this wasn't a metaphor he needed to extend.

At the tea stall near his stop he had become a regular, which in practice meant that the man made his tea without asking and slid it across without looking up. Nobody knew his name. "Ginger chaya," the man once said to a new boy, nodding in Arun's direction as the entire explanation. It was the closest thing to being known that he had, and he took it home with him and kept it, like a coin.

Sometimes he had breakfast at a stall. Idlis steaming in a cloth bundle. Sambar, thin and bright. The man behind the counter split vadas open at a speed that was almost musical. The women serving water did it without looking up from their thoughts. The table was wiped, and wiped again, not because it was still dirty but because wiping is a substitute for doing something permanent. A man and a woman sat two stools away and argued about rent as if rent were a decision made by a friend who would listen if only addressed correctly. It wasn't a bitter argument. It had the rhythm of two people who could afford to exaggerate for effect. When the vadas arrived they stopped talking and ate with the urgency of people who enjoy the part of life that would survive even if words didn't. The woman wiped a dot of sambar off the man's lip with her thumb and then looked at her thumb as if it were a new object. He kissed it quickly. Arun paid and left an extra five rupees, a tax for having witnessed something.

On weekends he walked down to the river. Water makes you try to interpret it, and then it makes your interpretation look like a cup dipped into the sea. People came to watch it take its time. A wedding photographer was arranging a couple under a tree, the bride balanced on a root, the groom trying to look brave in shoes that weren't built for mud. Children flung bread at fish that didn't want it. A man carried his mother's handbag because her shoulder had given up on it. Teenagers in matching jeans took photographs with their heads tilted the same way. Arun stood at the railing and gripped it with both hands when the ground seemed to lean. It leaned because he had caught himself hoping he might end up in somebody's photograph by accident. He switched the thought off, the way you turn off a tap that's been dripping.

In the late evenings the city undid its top button. Balconies became stages. People watered their plants, and the water hitting the railings made a sound like a small rain in rehearsal. Radios played songs that had lived through too many summers to be hurt any more. In the lane below his window a woman waited for a man on a bike, and finally sat down with her back to the wall and her eyes closed. He arrived late. She stood up without a word, climbed on behind him, and laid her palm flat on his spine, the way you close a book. The bike's noise trailed away and folded back into the lane. Arun had been standing at the window so long that he began to suspect it was what he actually did for a living.

Festivals arrived on schedule. Lights strung between houses became a kind of public courage. A procession went by with a deity decked in colours that expected applause. Drums argued with chests. Firecrackers rehearsed their small quarrels with the sky. Arun stood in the crowd, because crowds let you feel alone more honestly. He kept his arms at his sides and let the music walk through him without taking anything he couldn't afford to lose. A child on his left grabbed hold of his trouser pocket by mistake, believing him to be a father's shape. The mistake lasted two seconds. The child let go like a leaf.

He went to the cinema when the quiet of the flat got too much. The hall was darkness arranged in rows. Popcorn smell. Armrests slightly damp, and not asking for your confidence. On screen people found each other because the story required it. Their lines had already been written, so desire looked like fate. A man two seats away whispered into his partner's hair. A phone lit up three rows behind and made a bright rectangle that nagged at the dark. The audience gasped on cue at a twist nobody needed. Arun watched and allowed himself the discipline of going back to the lane alone afterwards, with no story to explain why. It felt correct to come out into the night without any additional fiction.

Sometimes in the metro a person set a bag down where he'd been standing a second earlier and said sorry without meeting his eye. Sometimes he caught his own reflection in the window and had to look away, because a reflection is only useful when it confirms something. Sometimes a child leaned his head on Arun's elbow because a moving train turns your body into furniture. He kept his elbow steady. The mother mouthed a thank you.

He saw a couple break up once on a bench outside a pharmacy. The woman's shoulders came up as if to protect her neck. The man kept shaking his head at a sum that wouldn't change however many times he shook it. They didn't cry. They used sentences that sounded like receipts. Arun thought it was unfair that he was there to see it, and then understood that fairness isn't something benches can tell you. He walked home carefully, and that night he took out a sheet of paper and wrote: *Dear Unknown, I saw an ending today that didn't need a witness.*
